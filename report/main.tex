\begin{abstract}
We study how the spectral shape of Muon's late-training update interacts with gradient noise in synthetic teacher--student regression. With separately tuned learning rates, Muon achieves lower excess risk than SGD and AdamW after 6000 steps at all four batch sizes, but a CPU timing benchmark shows that this advantage does not always carry over to equal estimated time. In paired branches from common checkpoints, the $p=-0.25$ update improves on continued exact Muon in all eight conditions. An exploratory extension finds that $p=-0.5$ is best among the tested exponents at the larger batch sizes. An initially incomplete tuning search changed the apparent ranking, illustrating the importance of tuning step size for each update shape. Our results are limited to one synthetic task, three evaluation seeds per branch condition, and the measured noise range.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
% Budget: 0.7 page, about 380 words, no figures.
% Takeaway: the question is open, and it can be tested here.
% - Muon flattens the singular values of each hidden-layer update. The brief asks whether boosting weak
%   directions helps, or amplifies noise.
% - Late training: Xie (2026) says switch to momentum SGD when noise dominates; DynMuon (Wu et al., 2026)
%   says flatten even more (p = -0.25).
% - Our question: does the best late-phase exponent p in U S^p V^T depend on gradient noise?
% - Contributions (3 bullets): fairly tuned per-step and per-second baselines; a pre-registered branch
%   test over 8 noise conditions; an exploratory follow-up that brackets p*.
Muon transforms the momentum updates of matrix-valued hidden weights. Its practical version uses a short Newton--Schulz iteration to approximately flatten the spectrum \citep{jordan2024muon}. Increasing the relative weight of weak directions may help optimisation, but may also amplify gradient noise. \citet{xie2026noise} analyse a noise-dominated regime where response-matched momentum can avoid a nonlinear residual introduced by orthogonalisation. \citet{wu2026dynmuon} instead motivate mildly negative powers late in training. These arguments address different settings and do not determine the outcome on our task.

We investigate whether modifying Muon's spectral update improves late-stage optimisation on a synthetic teacher--student regression task. We first compare Muon with SGD and AdamW under fixed training-step budgets and estimated computation-time budgets. We then compare separately tuned spectral exponents from shared checkpoints across batch sizes and momentum settings. A gradient-noise indicator measured at the switch helps us explore whether the observed differences are associated with noise, although this design does not isolate noise as their cause. Exploratory controls and additional exponents investigate the sensitivity of the results to step-size selection.

The initial experiment protocol specified three hypotheses:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{H1: Preferred exponent.} Higher gradient noise favours larger late-phase spectral exponents. We therefore expected larger exponents to perform better at smaller batch sizes or lower momentum, and non-positive exponents to be preferred at larger batch sizes or higher momentum.

    \item \textbf{H2: Switching to momentum SGD.} Switching from Muon to an unnormalised momentum update, using Xie's matched learning-rate scale, reduces late-stage excess risk. We expected a larger benefit at smaller batch sizes and lower momentum.

    \item \textbf{H3: Noise indicator.} The signal-to-noise indicator $\delta$ measured at the switch predicts the preferred exponent: smaller $\delta$, indicating more noise relative to signal, favours larger exponents.
\end{enumerate}
These hypotheses motivated the original branch experiment. The additional negative exponents and frozen-hidden-layer control were introduced after inspecting earlier results and are treated as exploratory.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
% Budget: 0.8 page, about 440 words.
% Takeaway: what p means, and what each theory predicts.
% - The Muon update: EMA momentum, the Nesterov input N, Newton-Schulz ~ polar factor U V^T, the shape
%   factor sqrt(max(1, m/n)). Equation (1) below gives the family studied.
% - p = 0 is exact Muon, p = 1 normalised momentum SGD, p < 0 boosts weak directions further.
% - Xie's indicator delta (signal over noise, per layer) and the matched switch; DynMuon's late p = -0.25.
% - Why step sizes must be tuned per p: Shumaylov et al. (2026). READ BEFORE CITING (abstract only).
For a hidden weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with Nesterov momentum input
$N = U S V^\top$ (thin SVD, numerical rank $r$), we study the update family
\begin{equation}
  D_p = \frac{\sqrt{r}}{\lVert U S^{p} V^\top \rVert_F}\; U S^{p} V^\top ,
  \qquad
  W \leftarrow W - \eta \sqrt{\max(1, m/n)}\; D_p .
  \label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
The exponent $p$ controls how the update distributes its strength across the singular directions of the momentum input $N$. Before transformation, a larger singular value indicates a direction with a stronger contribution to $N$. For the retained singular values, $p=1$ preserves their relative magnitudes, producing a Frobenius-normalised momentum-like update. Setting $p=0$ makes them equal, giving the exact polar update used by idealised Muon. A negative exponent gives relatively greater weight to directions with smaller singular values. These weak directions may contain useful remaining signal, but they may also contain noise.

To separate spectral shape from overall update magnitude, we normalise each direction $D_p$ to Frobenius norm $\sqrt{r}$ and tune the learning rate separately for each arm. Before applying the power, we discard singular values at or below $s_1\max(m,n)\epsilon$, where $\epsilon$ is float32 machine precision and $r$ is the number retained. This prevents negative powers from strongly amplifying numerically negligible singular values.

The normalised $p=1$ update differs from the separate Xie-style arm, which uses an unnormalised momentum direction with a matched learning-rate scale. Practical NS5 uses five Newton--Schulz iterations to approximate the polar transformation; its resulting spectrum differs from the exact-SVD update \citep{jordan2024muon}.

Xie's analysis motivates the signal-to-noise indicator $\delta$ and a matched switch \citep{xie2026noise}; DynMuon schedules the exponent across training \citep{wu2026dynmuon}. Our fixed-exponent branches do not implement the complete DynMuon schedule. Spectral shape and step size can be confounded, motivating separate tuning for each arm \citep{shumaylov2026special}.

\section{Setup and protocol}\label{sec:setup}
% Budget: 1.5 pages including Table 1 (about 0.2 page): about 700 words.
% Takeaway: the comparison is fair, and the brief's three rules are met:
%   (R1) same models and data, comparable tuning, stated budgets; (R2) per step and per time reported
%   separately; (R3) which parameters get Muon.
% - Task A: teacher-student regression with a fresh minibatch every step; input covariance with
%   eigenvalues ~ 1/i; label noise sigma_y = 0.5. Excess risk against the noiseless teacher on 16,384
%   fixed inputs, so the optimum is known.
% - Student: tanh MLP 128-256-256-256-16. (R3) Only the two 256 x 256 hidden matrices get the Muon
%   family; the first and last layers and the biases use AdamW at E1's best rate for that B.
% - Branch design: exact Muon for 3000 steps (E2, 5 seeds); every arm continues from the same checkpoint
%   with the same future data, so comparisons are paired.
% - (R1) Tuning: E1, 5 learning rates per optimiser and B. E3/F1, a multiplier per arm from {0.5, 1, 2},
%   extended by halving or doubling while the best is on an edge, within [1/64, 64]. Tuning seeds
%   {0, 1}; evaluation seeds {2, 3, 4}.
% - Statistics: paired 95% t-intervals against p = 0; p* with a bootstrap over seeds; the H1 contrast C.
% - Pre-registration: protocol fixed 20 Oct; a dated change log records the extension rule, the stopping
%   rule, the frozen control, F1 and the timing benchmark.
% - Budget: 1179 training runs, all on one CPU in float32 (bf16 is about 200-400x slower on this CPU).
We study synthetic teacher--student regression. Inputs are sampled from a 128-dimensional Gaussian distribution with covariance eigenvalues proportional to $1/i$, rescaled so that their sum is 128. A fixed randomly initialised teacher network has widths $128$--$64$--$64$--$16$, tanh hidden activations, and a linear output layer. Its outputs are standardised using estimated per-output means and standard deviations. Training labels add independent Gaussian noise with standard deviation $0.5$ to these outputs, and a fresh minibatch is generated at every training step.

The student has widths $128$--$256$--$256$--$256$--$16$, with tanh hidden activations and a linear output layer. In Muon-based runs, only the two $256\times256$ hidden weight matrices receive Muon or its variants. The input and output weight matrices and all biases use AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw}. The standalone AdamW and SGD baselines apply their respective optimiser to every student parameter. Weight decay is zero in all experiments.

We evaluate predictions on 16,384 fixed inputs using noiseless teacher outputs as targets. The mean squared prediction difference, averaged over inputs and output coordinates, estimates excess risk above the Bayes predictor. Using noiseless targets avoids having to estimate and subtract the label-noise floor. Our main outcome is tail excess risk: the average of evaluation measurements in the final 10\% of each training run or continuation branch.

In E1, each optimiser and batch size starts with five learning rates; the SGD grid is extended at $B=1024$. E1 reports the same two seeds used to select rates, so selection can make those outcomes optimistic. For E3, exact Muon trains for 3000 steps before branching. Branches from the same condition and seed share the checkpoint and subsequent data stream, so their differences are paired. Each arm is tuned on seeds 0 and 1 and evaluated on seeds 2--4. Tuning begins at multipliers $\{0.5,1,2\}$ and extends the grid if the selected value is on an edge, so trial counts differ across cells. Table~\ref{tab:multipliers} gives selected multipliers. Paired $t$-intervals over three evaluation seeds are fragile.

The E3 question, initial arms and comparisons were specified in the protocol; the tuning extension procedure and bounded stopping rule were subsequently documented during tuning. The frozen control and F1 were added after inspection and are exploratory. The timing procedure was changed to five-pass medians after a noisy single pass. The dated change log records these decisions. The per-layer indicator $\delta=\|\widehat{E G}\|_F/(\widehat\sigma_N\sqrt{mn})$ uses 32 fresh minibatch gradients, bias correction of the squared mean-gradient norm, and a momentum-noise calculation assuming stationarity and independent gradient noise. We average the estimate across both hidden matrices and seeds.

At 6000 steps, exact Muon has approximately 37.5, 25.2, 29.1 and 45.1\% lower tail excess risk than the better baseline, SGD, at $B=16,64,256,1024$, respectively; NS5 is close to exact Muon (Figure~\ref{fig:baselines}). \section{Pre-registered test: the late-phase exponent under noise}\label{sec:e3}
% Budget: 1.5 pages including Figure 2 and Table 2 (about 0.6 page together): about 480 words.
% Takeaway: flattening more wins everywhere; H1 and H2 fail as stated.
% - Noise at the switch: delta from 0.69 (B = 16, beta = 0.9) to 4.09 (B = 1024, beta = 0.95); only one
%   condition is clearly noise-dominated (Table 2, Figure A1).
% - Changes against continuing Muon (evaluation seeds): p = -0.25 -2.4 to -7.6%; p = 0.5 +8.6 to +21.2%;
%   p = 1 +11.7 to +41.5%; Xie +11.8 to +37.4%; NS5 -1.3 to +1.7%. p* = -0.25 in all 8 conditions.
% - Verdicts: H1 contradicted, C = +0.00877 [+0.00833, +0.00922] (beta 0.9) and +0.00657 [+0.00617,
%   +0.00698] (beta 0.95); H2 not supported; H3 not testable (p* constant).
% - Disclose the design flaw: C compares absolute gaps, and excess risk is about 14x larger at B = 16.
Table~\ref{tab:conditions} and Figure~\ref{fig:e3} show the paired evaluation. Relative to continuing exact Muon, $p=-0.25$ improves tail risk by approximately 2.4--7.6\% across conditions, while $p=1$ and the Xie-style switch increase it by about 12--41\% and 12--37\%, respectively. NS5 remains close to exact Muon. The best tested exponent in the original E3 grid is $-0.25$ in all eight conditions.

The planned H1 contrast compares absolute $p=1$ minus $p=-0.25$ risk gaps at $B=16$ and $B=1024$; H1 predicts a negative contrast. The saved analysis instead gives $C=0.00877$ [0.00833, 0.00922] for $\beta=0.9$ and $0.00657$ [0.00617, 0.00698] for $\beta=0.95$ (Welch 95\% intervals; the gaps within each batch size are paired over seeds). This contradicts the specified direction, but absolute gaps are difficult to interpret when risk scales differ substantially across batches. The switch hypothesis is not supported in these conditions. The original H3 association cannot be assessed using the E3 best exponent because it is constant. Estimated $\delta$ ranges from 0.69 to 4.09; the strongly noise-dominated $\delta\ll1$ regime is not reached, so these results do not refute the switch in that regime.

\section{Exploratory follow-ups}\label{sec:exploratory}
% Budget: 1.1 pages including Figure 3 (about 0.35 page): about 400 words.
% Takeaway: the optimum shifts mildly with batch size, and the SGD-like arms lose for an identifiable
% reason. Say once at the top, and in every caption, that this section is exploratory.
% - F1: p* = -0.25 at B <= 64 and -0.5 at B >= 256, both beta. p = -0.5 minus p = -0.25: +1.4 to +4.1%
%   at B <= 64, -1.3 to -2.4% at B >= 256; 7 of 8 intervals exclude 0. rho(delta, p*) = -0.76 (exact
%   permutation p = 0.029); rho(log B, p*) = -0.89; momentum changes delta by 56-60% without moving p*.
%   The new arms needed x8-x32 at B >= 64; at x2, p = -0.5 looked up to 43% worse than Muon.
% - Frozen control: at B = 16 the SGD-like arms are no better than freezing the hidden layers (p = 1:
%   0.0672 vs 0.0675 at beta = 0.9); at B = 1024 they clearly beat it (0.0056 vs 0.0076).
% - Scale-free contrast: -0.199 and -0.163 (H1's direction); the gain of p = -0.25 over Muon rises with
%   delta (rho = +0.88).
\textbf{These follow-ups were added after inspecting earlier results and are exploratory.} Since $-0.25$ was at the boundary of the original exponent grid, F1 added $p=-0.5$ and $-0.75$. Among the tested exponents, $-0.25$ has the lowest mean risk at $B\leq64$ and $-0.5$ at $B\geq256$, for both momenta (Figure~\ref{fig:f1}). The $-0.5$ versus $-0.25$ difference is modest: approximately 1.4--4.1\% worse at the smaller batches and 1.3--2.4\% better at the larger batches. Three paired evaluation seeds and an adaptively chosen follow-up limit certainty. A scale-free contrast points in H1's direction; it is a different, exploratory quantity and does not replace the planned absolute contrast.

An early F1 selection was made before tuning extensions were complete. At the provisional multiplier $\times2$, $p=-0.5$ appeared up to 43\% worse than Muon in some conditions. Extending the tuning grid and selecting again changed the ranking; the scripts now guard against unfinished or stale selections. The exploratory frozen-hidden-layer control probes very small step sizes chosen for SGD-like branches. At $B=16$, $\beta=0.9$, mean risks are approximately 0.0672 for $p=1$ and 0.0675 when hidden matrices are frozen; at $B=1024$ they are 0.0056 and 0.0076. These comparisons suggest little hidden-layer contribution at small batch but do not establish its cause.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
% Budget: 0.9 page, about 500 words.
% Takeaway: what this means for using Muon, and how far it generalises.
% - Against the two theories: DynMuon's mildly negative p holds across a 64x range of batch size; Xie's
%   regime (delta << 1 near the optimum) was never reached (smallest delta 0.69).
% - Why SGD-like steps fail at small batch (frozen control). Hypothesis, labelled as such: the
%   hidden-layer gradients are ill-conditioned, so a step large enough for the weak directions is too
%   large for the strong ones.
% - Step size and shape are coupled: more negative p needs larger multipliers; this decided both the
%   pilot and F1.
% - Limitations: one synthetic task and a small MLP; CPU float32, not bf16; weight decay 0; 3 evaluation
%   seeds; p grid in steps of 0.25; equal steps across B (larger batches see more data); timing is
%   hardware- and load-dependent; F1 not pre-registered. Without E5, H2 is "not supported in our noise
%   regime", not "refuted".
The results favour some additional flattening over continuing exact Muon on this task, consistent with the qualitative late-phase motivation of \citet{wu2026dynmuon}. They do not establish that a fixed negative exponent or a switch to SGD generalises to other tasks. The Xie-style switch is not supported in the tested range, which does not reach $\delta\ll1$ \citep{xie2026noise}. One possible explanation for weak small-batch SGD-like progress is ill-conditioning: a step small enough for strong directions may be ineffective for weak ones. The frozen control alone does not test this mechanism.

Limitations include one synthetic task and small MLP, CPU float32 rather than accelerator timing, no weight decay, three branch evaluation seeds, and a coarse exponent grid. Equal steps across batches process unequal numbers of examples. Noisy timing, E1 tuning-seed reuse, protocol amendments and exploratory F1 restrict conclusions. The eight batch-size and momentum conditions are not independent replications of a causal noise intervention. Future work could target a lower measured $\delta$, evaluate selected E1 rates on new seeds and tune for a fixed time budget.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
% Budget: 0.2 page, about 110 words. Three lines: the main answer, the exploratory refinement, the
% method lesson (per-arm step-size tuning decided every answer).
On this synthetic task, Muon improved risk per step but not consistently per estimated CPU second. A mildly negative exponent improved the late-phase outcome across the original grid, and an exploratory extension suggested a small batch-dependent shift among negative exponents. The tuning history shows why comparisons of update shapes require careful step-size selection.
